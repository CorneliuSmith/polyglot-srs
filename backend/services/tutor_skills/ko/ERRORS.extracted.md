# Common learner errors — Korean (ko)

_Extracted from source documents; review before folding into ERRORS.md._

## Korean sentence structure: Subject – Object – Verb
- Placing the verb before the object as in English word order.
- Ending a Korean sentence with a noun or an adverb instead of a verb/adjective.
- Adding an object (with ~을/를) to a sentence whose predicate is an adjective.

## Topic particle ~는/은
- Using ~는 after a consonant (e.g. 집는) or ~은 after a vowel (e.g. 나은).
- Trying to translate the particle into English.

## Object particle ~를/을
- Attaching ~를/을 in a sentence predicated by 이다 (나는 남자를 이다 is very wrong).
- Attaching ~를/을 to the thing you 'have' with 있다.

## Particle ~에 (place or time)
- Putting the ~에 phrase at the end of the sentence, after the verb.
- Forgetting ~에 on time expressions.

## 이다: to be
- Writing 이다 as a separate word instead of attached to the noun.
- Using the object particle before 이다 (나는 남자를 이다).
- Choosing 예요 after a consonant-final noun or 이에요 after a vowel-final noun.
- Using the unconjugated forms of Lessons 1–4 in real conversation – Koreans will most likely not understand them.

## Demonstratives 이, 그, 저
- Using 저 for something that was simply mentioned earlier – use 그.
- Assuming 그 and 저 are interchangeable because both translate as 'that'.

## 이것, 그것, 저것 (pronouns)
- Writing a space inside the compound (이 것 instead of 이것).
- Using 이것/그것/저것 directly before another noun instead of 이/그/저.

## 있다 with ~이/가: to have, to be at a location
- Saying 나는 펜을 있다 – 있다 is an adjective and cannot take an object particle.
- Mixing up which allomorph goes with a consonant-final noun (펜이, not 펜가).

## 나 vs 저: two words for 'I'
- Using 나 in a polite sentence with ~요/~습니다 endings.

## Present Tense Conjugation of 이다 / 아니다
- Writing 아니예요 instead of the standard 아니에요 (even native speakers mix these up).
- Using ~예요 after a consonant-final noun (선생님예요) instead of ~이에요.
- Marking the noun before 아니다 with ~를/을; it must take ~이/가 (학생이 아니에요).
- Trying to leave a modifying adjective like 좋은 at the end of a sentence instead of adding a noun + 이다 (나는 좋은 사람이다).

## Past Tense Conjugation of 이다 / 아니다
- Writing 아니였어/아니였어요 instead of 아니었어/아니었어요; ~였~ only exists as a contraction of 이 + 었 and is never added to a stem on its own.
- Using ~였 after a consonant-final noun (선생님였어) instead of ~이었.
- Forgetting that the contraction ~였 is optional, not required, after a vowel.

## Future Tense with ~ㄹ/을 것이다
- Ending a sentence with the bare ~ㄹ/을 form (나는 먹을) — a noun such as 것/거 must follow it.
- Conjugating 이다 into the future too (것이겠다); 이다 stays in the present because ~ㄹ/을 already carries the future meaning.
- Choosing the wrong copula after 것 vs 거: 것이에요 (consonant) but 거예요 (vowel); the spelling 할 거예요 is often confused with 이에요/에요.
- Adding ~을 to a vowel-final stem or ~ㄹ to a consonant-final stem.

## Irregular Stems + ~ㄹ/을
- Writing 돕을/돕아 forms with ~ㄹ/을: ㅂ becomes 우 (도울) before vowels other than ~아/어.
- Adding ~을 to a ㄹ-final stem (길을) instead of dropping ㄹ and reattaching ~ㄹ (길).
- Changing ㅡ and 르 stems when ~ㄹ/을 is added; they stay as 잠글, 다를.

## Future with 되다 (to become) vs 이다
- Marking the resulting noun with ~를/을 before 되다; it must take ~이/가.
- Assuming 일 in 의사일 거예요 is the noun 일; it is 이다 + ~ㄹ.
- Using 이겠다 for 'will be' — it sounds awkward; use ~ㄹ/을 것이다 or 되다.
- Negating an 이다 sentence with 안/~지 않다 instead of 아니다 (의사가 아닐 거야).

## ~ㄹ/을 것이다 for Guessing (probably)
- Using this guessing form about yourself — the speaker normally guesses about someone/something else.
- Adding future time words ('next year', 'in a few months') to a guessing sentence, which changes the meaning to a real future.
- Thinking 일 is a separate noun rather than 이다 + ~ㄹ.

## Sino-Korean Numbers
- Putting spaces inside a number (오천 육백); numbers are written without spaces.
- Trying to build large numbers in thousands instead of ten-thousands (만).
- Using Sino-Korean numbers where pure Korean numbers are required, e.g. counting things or telling the hour.

## Pure Korean Numbers and Counters
- Forgetting to shorten 하나/둘/셋/넷/스물 to 한/두/세/네/스무 before a counter.
- Omitting the counter altogether (사람 둘 instead of 사람 두 명).
- Writing the number and counter together (한개) instead of with a space (한 개).
- Using Sino-Korean numbers to count things or to say the hour.
- Over-using the 두 명의 사람 order; noun + number + counter is far more common.

## Counters with numbers (개, 명, 대, 잔)
- Dropping particles too early: beginners should practise attaching 를/을 or 가/이 to the counter before imitating the particle-less spoken style.
- Forgetting that 하나/둘/셋/넷 become 한/두/세/네 in front of a counter (두 개, not 둘 개).
- Writing a pure Korean number and its counter together with no space (두개 instead of 두 개).

## Saying zero: 영 and 공
- Reading phone-number zeros as 영 instead of 공.
- Using 공 for scores or temperatures, where 영 is required.

## 번 – counter for times (how many times)
- Attaching 를/을 to 번 — it is already an adverb.
- Using Sino-Korean numbers (이 번 for 'twice' instead of 두 번).

## Telling time: 시 and 분
- Using Sino-Korean numbers for the hour (이 시 instead of 두 시).
- Using pure Korean numbers for the minutes (삼십 분, not 서른 분).
- Forgetting 에 when saying at what time something happens.

## Age with 살
- Using Sino-Korean numbers for a young person's age (이십육 살 instead of 스물여섯 살).

## 번째: ordinal numbers
- Saying 한 번째 instead of 첫 번째 for 'first'.
- Forgetting that 2–4 shorten (두/세/네 번째, not 둘/셋/넷 번째).

## Shortened ordinals: 첫째, 둘째, 셋째, 넷째
- Using the short forms with ordinary nouns: 저는 셋째 문을 열었어요 is understandable but awkward — say 세 번째 문.
- Using 넷째 사람 instead of 네 번째 사람 when simply counting people in a sequence.

## 마지막 (final) vs 지난 (previous)
- Using 마지막 for 'last night/last week' when you mean the previous one — use 지난.
- Treating 마지막 as an adjective and trying to conjugate it.

## 처음 – at first / for the first time
- Using 첫 번째 where the adverbial 처음 (for the first time) is needed.

## 동안 – 'for' a period of time
- Adding 에 after 동안 (동안에 for this basic 'for X time' meaning).
- Choosing the wrong number system for the time counter in front of 동안.

## Counting hours: 시간 vs 시
- Using 시 for a duration (세 시 동안 instead of 세 시간 동안).
- Using Sino-Korean numbers with 시간.

## Counting seconds: 초
- Using pure Korean numbers with 초 (열 초 instead of 십 초).

## 지난/다음 + time words (last time / next time)
- Saying 지난 분 / 지난 초 — use 마지막 for 'the last minute/second' of a sequence.
- Reading 지난 시간 / 다음 시간 as 'last hour / next hour' instead of 'last time / next time'.
- Attaching ~에 to 수업 (수업에 듣다) instead of the object particle ~을.

## Korean days: 일, 날, 하루
- Using pure Korean numbers to count days (세 일 instead of 삼 일).
- Using 일 on its own to mean 'day' — alone it means 'work'.
- Trying to count with 날 (세 날) instead of 일.

## Korean weeks: 주 and 주일
- Using pure Korean numbers with 주 (두 주 instead of 이 주 / 2 주).

## Korean months: 달 vs 개월
- Mixing the number systems: 이 달 for 'two months' or 두 개월 for 'two months'.

## Month names (1월 – 12월)
- Reading 6월 as 육월 and 10월 as 십월.
- Using pure Korean numbers for calendar months (네 월 instead of 사월).

## Pure Korean vs Sino-Korean numbers with time counters
- Mixing systems: 열 초, 세 일, 두 개월, 이 달 (for 'two months') are all wrong.
- Assuming the choice is arbitrary instead of checking whether the word is of Chinese origin.

## Passive verbs with ~아/어 있다 (describing a state)
- Marking the subject of a passive/state sentence with ~을/를 (문을 닫혀 있어요) — passive verbs cannot take an object; use ~이/가.
- Assuming every passive verb can take ~아/어 있다: 나는 속아 있다 is not natural because 'tricked' is not a lasting state.
- Confusing 문이 닫혔어요 (the door got closed) with 문이 닫혀 있어요 (the door is closed).

## Passive/active pairs with 나다 and 내다
- Using the present tense where Korean uses the past: 컴퓨터가 고장 나요 for 'the computer is broken' sounds wrong; say 고장 났어요.
- Giving 나다 an object with ~을/를 — it is passive, so use ~이/가.
- Inventing a 내다 partner for every 나다 word (어긋내다 does not exist).

## 나다 with nouns (기억이 나다, 화가 나다, 땀이 나다…)
- Saying 저는 그것을 기억나요 — 나다 is passive, so it must be 그것이.
- Using the present tense for states like anger, sweat or memory where Korean prefers the past (화가 났어요).
- Assuming the past-for-present pattern also works in negatives; 기억 안 났어 refers to the past only.

## Making normal verbs passive with ~아/어지다
- Contracting 주어지다 to 줘지다 — the correct form is 주어지다.
- Forgetting the ㅅ-irregular change in 짓다 → 지어지다.
- Overusing passives: Korean prefers active sentences.

## Turning adjectives into verbs with ~아/어하다 (좋아하다, 싫어하다)
- Treating 좋아 or 싫어 as nouns ('likeness'/'dislikeness') — they are only conjugated forms of 좋다/싫다.
- Using ~이/가 with the verb 좋아하다 when the object particle ~을/를 is expected.

## Subject – Object – Adjective form: ~이/가 with adjectives and passive verbs
- Saying 저는 김치를 좋아요 — an adjective cannot act on an object; use 김치가 좋아요 or the verb 좋아해요.
- Saying 저는 한국 음식을 그리워요 instead of 한국 음식이 그리워요.
- Forgetting that passive verbs follow the same rule: 저는 땀을 나요 is wrong.

## The many uses of 들다
- Translating 마음에 들다 literally as 'a picture enters my heart' instead of understanding it as 'to like'.
- Expecting one English equivalent to cover every use of 들다.

## Korean compound verbs (verb stem + ~아/어 + verb)
- Forgetting the ~아/어 linker (writing 들가다 instead of 들어가다).
- Trying to translate a compound word by one of its halves only.
- Mixing up 나가다 (go out) and 나오다 (come out) — the difference is the direction relative to the point of reference.

## 가져오다 vs 가져가다 (bring vs take)
- Thinking 가져가다 is for the future and 가져오다 for the past — it depends on direction, not tense.
- Ignoring the listener's point of reference when someone asks you to bring something to them (they say 가져오다).

## Comparing with 다르다/비슷하다/같다 and the particle ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고
- Saying 우리는 같아요 when nothing is being compared — Korean prefers 우리는 똑같아요.
- Looking for three different particles because English uses 'from', 'to' and 'as' — one particle (~와/과/랑/이랑/하고) covers all three.
- Conjugating 다르다 regularly: the polite form is 달라요, not '다라요'.

## Describing nouns with adjective phrases (다른/같은/비슷한)
- Using the plain dictionary form in front of the noun (비슷하다 남자) instead of the modifier form 비슷한.
- Forgetting that the ~와/과 phrase stays in front of the modifier: 남자친구와 비슷한 남자, not 비슷한 남자와 남자친구.

## 또 다르다 = another (vs. 다르다 = other)
- Treating 또 다르다 as a single vocabulary item — it is 또 + 다르다.
- Using plain 다른 when you mean 'one more of the same kind already under discussion'; add 또.

## 걸리다: one word, many meanings
- Assuming a Korean word with many English translations has 'many unrelated meanings' — the meanings are often one core idea.
- Using ~을/를 with 걸리다: it does not take an object (감기를 걸렸어요 is wrong).

## Talking about pain and sickness: 아프다 and ~에 걸리다
- Using 는/은 on the body part: say 팔이 아파요, not 팔은 아파요.
- Using the present tense for a cold you currently have: Korean says 감기에 걸렸어요 (past).
- Copying English and saying 'my arm is sick' — 아프다 covers both 'sore' and 'sick', but ~에 걸리다 is for illnesses.

## Korean suffix: ~적/~적으로/~적이다
- Assuming ~적 can be attached to any noun — only specific Chinese-origin nouns allow it, and you must learn them one by one.
- Trying to conjugate bare ~적: it is a noun, so nothing is conjugated (민주적 정부). Only ~적이다 is conjugated.
- Always adding English '-al': 과학적 is 'scientific' and 충동적 is 'impulsive'.
- In speech, preferring bare ~적 before a noun; ~적인 is the more natural choice.

## Korean suffix: ~스럽다
- Attaching ~스럽다 to any noun — only learned words allow it.
- Conjugating it regularly: it is ㅂ-irregular (사랑스러워요, not '사랑스럽어요').
- Assuming ~스럽다 and ~하다 forms mean the same thing (실망스럽다 ≠ 실망하다).

## 실망하다 vs. 실망스럽다 (with ~에게/한테 and ~에)
- Marking the person or thing with ~을/를: 저는 학생을 실망했어요 is incorrect because 실망하다 takes no object.
- Using ~에게 for a thing or ~에 for a person — people take ~에게/한테, non-people take ~에.
- Using 실망스럽다 to describe your own feelings directly; it describes the disappointing thing (often left out).

## Adjectives with ~스럽다
- Don't mark the thing you're disappointed in with ~을/를: use 학생에게 실망했어요 (person) or 영화에 실망했어요 (thing).
- 실망스럽다/만족스럽다 describe the thing, not the person's emotion; use 실망하다/만족하다 for emotions.
- ~스럽다 words are ㅂ-irregular, so 만족스럽어요 is wrong — it must be 만족스러워요.
- An adverb in ~게 cannot be followed by 이다 (자연스럽게이다 ✗).

## Adverbs with ~스럽게
- Never add 이다 to a ~게 adverb (나의 머리 색깔은 자연스럽게이다 ✗).
- Learners sometimes place the ~게 adverb after the verb; it belongs before the verb.

## Sequential ~아/어서
- Never conjugate the verb before ~아/어서 to the past tense (갔어서 ✗).
- Using ~고 instead of ~아/어서 with 가다/오다 for 'go/come somewhere and do something' is wrong.
- 저는 눕고 책을 읽었어요 means you lay down, got up, then read — use 누워서 for reading while lying down.

## I want to: ~고 싶다
- 싶다 is an adjective: 나는 먹고 싶는다 is wrong — say 나는 먹고 싶다.
- Don't attach ~고 싶다 to adjectives; use ~아/어지다 to say 'want to become …'.
- Use 원하다 for wanting a noun and ~고 싶다 for wanting to do an action.
- 결혼하고 싶다 needs 'with' (나랑 결혼하고 싶었어), not ~을/를 — this is why Koreans often say 'marry with you' in English.

## ~이/가 and ~는/은 Revisited
- Learners often use ~이/가 for general statements; general facts take ~는/은.
- In comparison sentences, both compared nouns need ~는/은, not ~이/가.

## ~는/은 vs ~이/가: general statements and immediate experience
- Saying 비는 와요 for 'it is raining' — rain falling is always a specific, experienced event, so 비가 와요 is required.
- Trying to translate 는/은 and 이/가 into English words (like 'the' and 'a'); their difference is nuance, not meaning.
- Using 는/은 with something that is not generally true (밥은 딱딱하다) unless you really mean a contrast with something else.

## Present Progressive: ~고 있다
- Attaching ~고 있다 to an adjective (저는 행복하고 있어요 is wrong); adjectives cannot 'progress'.
- Using the past ~고 있었어요 where a simple past is more natural in Korean.
- Adding ~고 있다 to position verbs: 앉고 있다 means you are in the process of bending down to sit, not that you are seated.
- Translating 알고 있다 as 'I am knowing' instead of 'I know'.
- Using 가지고 있다 for people (나는 여자 친구를 가지고 있어 sounds like 'I possess a girlfriend').

## ~아/어 있다: being in a state
- Using 앉고 있다 / 서고 있다 when you mean 'I am seated' / 'I am standing' — use 앉아 있다 / 서 있다.
- Confusing 살고 있다 (to live in a place) with 살아 있다 (to be alive).

## 가지고 있다: to have / to possess
- Saying 나는 펜을 가져 for 'I have a pen'; use 가지고 있어 instead.
- Using 가지고 있다 with people (나는 여자 친구를 가지고 있어 sounds like 'I possess a girlfriend').
- Forgetting that 있다 for possession takes ~이/가 on the thing possessed.

## 알고 있다 and 알았어 / 알겠습니다
- Translating 알고 있다 word-for-word as 'I am knowing'.
- Using 알았어 in a formal situation instead of 알겠습니다.

## Adjective + ~아/어지다: to become (adjective)
- Writing the diary form as 추워지다 instead of 추워진다 — the ~아/어지다 construction is a verb.
- Attaching ~고 있다 or ~고 싶다 straight onto an adjective; add ~아/어지다 first.
- Overusing the present tense of ~아/어지다 when the past or future is what you mean.

## Tenses of ~아/어지다 (example: 추워지다)
- Conjugating ~아/어지다 as if it were still an adjective (e.g. the diary form).

## 더: 'more' in Korean sentences
- Placing 더 after the adjective instead of before it.
- Trying to change the adjective itself (as in English '-er'); Korean only adds 더.

## 동안: 'for' a time and 'during' a noun
- Using 동안 before the time expression instead of after it.

## Comparatives with ~보다 and 더
- ~보다 is a particle: attach it directly to the noun with no space and no separate word for 'than'.
- Learners attach ~보다 to the thing being described; it must go on the standard of comparison (친구보다 = than my friend).
- Don't put 더 at the end of the sentence — it comes before the verb, adjective or adverb it modifies.
- With counters, the counter phrase comes before 더: 책을 두 권 더 읽었어요.

## 덜 – Less
- Sentences with 덜 sound less natural than the flipped version with 더 — prefer 더 with the opposite subject order.
- 덜 is an adverb, so it can never take particles.

## 낫다 – to be better
- Writing 낫아요 instead of 나아요 — 낫다 is ㅅ-irregular.
- Using 낫다 for a general statement where 좋다 belongs; 낫다 wants a comparison.
- Confusing 낫다 (to be better) with 낮다 (to be low).

## Superlatives with 가장/제일
- You cannot say 나는 가장 달려 — with verbs an adverb is needed (가장 빨리 달려), except with 좋아하다.
- Negative superlatives with ~지 않다/안 (가장 예쁘지 않은) are technically fine but sound unnatural; use an opposite word instead.
- 가장/제일 are adverbs and take no particles.

## 잘하다: to do something well
- Learners use a verb where a noun is needed: it is 공부를 잘하다, not 공부하다를 잘하다.
- Choose the right particle: 을 after a consonant, 를 after a vowel.

## 잘: well (adverb)
- 잘 must come before the verb, not after it.
- Note the spacing difference: 공부를 잘 해 and 공부를 잘해 sound almost identical but are written differently.
- Don't translate English literally — 잘 모르겠어요 is normal Korean even though 'I don't know well' is odd in English.

## 못하다: to do something poorly
- Don't use 못하다 with actions everyone can do (eating, walking) — it would claim your general ability is bad; use 못 instead.

## 못: poorly / couldn't
- Very common learner mistake: answering 안 들었어 ('I purposely didn't listen') when 못 들었어 ('I couldn't hear you') is meant.
- 안 / ~지 않다 carry no meaning of being prevented — don't use them when something stopped you.
- 못 is written separately before the verb (못 갔어요), unlike the verb 못하다.

## ~지 못하다
- Don't mix the two patterns into 공부를 하지 못하다 when you mean general inability — that is 공부를 못하다.
- ~지 못하다 attaches to the verb stem, so the tense goes on 못하다, not on the main verb.

## 잘못 vs 잘 못
- Spacing changes the meaning: 잘못 = mistake, 잘 못 = not well.
- Say 제 잘못 / 내 잘못, not 저의 잘못 / 나의 잘못.

## Asking yes/no questions with intonation
- Don't add an English-style auxiliary ('do/did/will') — nothing is added to the Korean sentence.
- Avoid inserting 당신/너 for 'you'; the subject is normally dropped when asking about the listener.

## Formal questions ~ㅂ/습니까?
- Never use ~습니다 with a question mark — the question form is ~ㅂ/습니까.
- Choosing the wrong allomorph: ~ㅂ니까 goes on vowel-final stems, ~습니까 on consonant-final stems.
- Past-tense stems always take ~습니까 (갔습니까), never ~ㅂ니까.

## Korean Questions ~니
- Forgetting to drop the ㄹ of a ㄹ-final stem: 만들니 is wrong, it must be 만드니.
- Trying to choose between ~아니/~어니: ~니 is a 'solid ㄴ' addition, there is only one form.
- Using ~니 to a superior; it is informal and sounds slightly feminine, so it is not a substitute for ~ㅂ/습니까.

## Solid ㄴ additions and the ㄹ irregular
- Keeping the ㄹ before a ㄴ-initial ending (살니, 팔나요) — the ㄹ must be dropped.
- Assuming every ending has an 아/어 choice; solid ㄴ endings have exactly one form.

## Korean Questions ~ㄴ/은가(요)
- Trying to use ~ㄴ/은가(요) in the past or future tense; it exists only in the present.
- Attaching it to action verb stems — it belongs with adjectives (and 이다).
- Choosing the wrong linker: consonant-final stems take ~은가, vowel-final stems take ~ㄴ가.

## Korean Questions ~나(요)
- Adding ~나(요) to 이다 or to ~ㄹ/을 것이다 (할 거나요?) — unnatural; use ~겠나요 for the future.
- Keeping the ㄹ of the stem (팔나요) instead of dropping it (파나요).
- Using ~나(요) with adjectives where ~ㄴ/은가(요) is more natural.

## Why (왜)
- Putting 왜 after the negative 안; the question word normally comes before other adverbs.
- Translating the reply 왜? literally as 'why?' when English would say 'what?'.

## When (언제)
- Confusing 언제 (when) with 어제 (yesterday).
- Always putting the question word at the front of the sentence; as an adverb it usually sits right before the verb.

## Where (어디)
- Saying 아빠가 어디야? / 그 만화책이 어디야? — for people and objects use 어디에 있어요? instead.
- Attaching ~부터 to 어디; it is not natural with places.

## Who (누구 / 누가)
- Putting 'who' at the front of the sentence regardless of its role, as in English.
- Using 누구가 for the subject instead of 누가.

## How (어떻게)
- Putting 어떻게 before an adjective or adverb ('how beautiful', 'how often') — use 얼마나 there.
- Using 뭐 for 'what do you think about…'; Korean uses 어떻게 생각해요?

## 어때? – asking for an opinion
- Attaching a particle to the thing being asked about; 점심 어때? is more natural than 점심이 어때?
- Always translating 어때 as 'how about'.

## What (뭐 / 무엇)
- Attaching ~를 to 뭐 in ordinary questions (뭐를 먹었어요?) — leave it bare.
- Using 무엇 before 이다 (이름이 무엇이야?) instead of 뭐.

## What (무슨) before a noun
- Treating 무슨 as an adjective that can be conjugated; it is a fixed word.
- Using 무슨 as a stand-alone pronoun; it must be followed by a noun.

## Which (어떤 / 어느)
- Using 어떤 where the answer is completely unpredictable (어떤 냄새야?) — 무슨 is the right choice there.
- Forgetting that 어떤 can also simply mean 'some/a certain', not only 'which'.

## 무슨, 어떤 and 어느: asking 'what' and 'which' before nouns
- Don't use 무슨 to ask about the type or characteristics of something (그 사람이 무슨 사람이야? is awkward).
- Don't use 무슨 when you are giving the listener options to choose from (무슨 쪽으로 가고 싶어요? is not said in Korean).
- Don't use 어떤 to ask about something whose answer you cannot predict at all (어떤 냄새야? only works if you want the smell described).
- Don't use 어느 for characteristics or for something unknown (어느 소리야?, 그 사람이 어느 사람이야? are awkward).

## 어떤 to mean 'some / a certain'
- 나는 무슨 책을 읽었어 and 나는 어느 책을 읽었어 are awkward — only 어떤 can express 'some book'.

## 몇 + counter: asking 'how many'
- Don't attach particles to 몇 + counter; put the particle on the noun instead (차가 몇 대 있어요? rather than 차 몇 대가 있어요?).
- 몇 대가 있어요 as a statement means 'I have some cars' — remember to raise your intonation at the end to make it a question.

## 얼마나: asking 'how (much)'
- Don't use 얼마나 when you are counting separate objects — use 몇 + counter (빵을 몇 개 먹었어요?).
- Avoid the 얼마나 많은 + noun pattern; 얼마나 (많이) + verb sounds far more natural.
- 얼마나 asks about extent/amount, while 어떻게 asks about manner or method — they are not interchangeable.

## 얼마: asking how much something costs
- Don't use 얼마나 to ask a price; 얼마예요? is the set expression.

## Using question words on their own
- Use 누가 (not 누구) when you want to know who did the action.

## ~는/은 and ~이/가 revisited: nuance and form
- Saying 내가 밥을 먹었어 out of the blue sounds like 'I am the one who ate rice!' — use 나는 밥을 먹었어 unless the subject really needs stressing.
- An English 'who' question does not automatically require ~이/가 in the answer: only when the question asks about the subject (누가), not the object (누구를).
- In state sentences like 문이 열려 있다, replacing ~이/가 with ~는/은 is only acceptable if you are making a comparison.

## ~기 전에 (before doing)
- Do not conjugate the verb before ~기 전에 for past or future — write 먹기 전에, never 먹었기 전에.
- Do not use ~는/은 on the sub-clause subject by default; ~이/가 is normal there (친구가 오기 전에…).
- Do not state the same subject twice (내가… 나는…); say 'I' only once when both clauses share the subject.

## ~ㄴ/은 후에 (after doing)
- Never add ~기 before 후 (먹기 후에 is wrong; use 먹은 후에).
- Choose ~은 only for consonant-final stems; a vowel-final stem takes ~ㄴ attached directly (가 → 간, not 가은).
- Do not put the past tense inside the clause; the sentence's tense comes from the main clause.

## Time expression + 후에 (later / from now)
- With 시간 (hours) use native Korean numbers (두 시간, 열 시간), not Sino-Korean ones.

## ~ㄴ/은 다음에 (after doing) — 다음 for 후
- With nouns, learners insert a verb form and say '수업인 다음에' instead of simply '수업 다음에'.
- Learners misapply the ㄹ-irregular, saying '살은 다음에' instead of '산 다음에'.
- Learners attach 다음에 to adjectives, e.g. '바쁜 다음에 만나요', when only action verbs work with this pattern.

## ~ㄴ/은 이래로 (since)
- 이래로 sounds bookish — in everyday speech use ~ㄴ/은 후에 for 'since'.

## 안 / 이내 (within a period of time)
- Learners drop the location particle and say '일주일 안 연락할게요' instead of '일주일 안에 연락할게요'.
- Learners confuse the negative 안 with the '안에' of time and put it before the verb, e.g. '삼 일 안 끝내요' meaning 'I won't finish in three days'.
- Learners use 동안 for a deadline, saying '한 시간 동안 끝낼게요' when they mean '한 시간 안에 끝낼게요'.

## 모든 + noun (every / all ___)
- 모든 cannot end a sentence or predicate a clause (there is no 모드다).
- 모든 must be followed by a noun; to say 'all' as an adverb use 다 or 모두 instead.

## 다 (all — adverb of completion)
- Never attach ~를 to 다 (나는 다 이해했어, not 나는 다를 이해했어).
- 다 stresses completion; 모든 + noun stresses 'each and every one', so 저는 모든 라면을 먹었어요 does not say you finished them.

## 모두 (all / everybody, everything)
- Don't attach particles to 모두 when it is used adverbially.
- In the simple Subject – 모두 – Verb pattern, 모두를 sounds unnatural; drop the ~를.

## Question word + ~나 (everywhere / every time / everyone)
- Do not add subject or object particles on top of ~나 (누구나가… is unnatural).

## Question word + ~ㄴ가 (something / somewhere / sometime / somebody)
- Keep ~에서 with 어딘가 when the verb requires it; only ~에 can be dropped.
- Don't attach ~에, ~까지 or ~부터 to 언젠가.

## 어느 + noun (some / an ambiguous ___)
- Using 어느 as a stand-alone pronoun: learners say '어느가 더 좋아요?' instead of '어느 것이 더 좋아요?' — 어느 must always be followed by a noun.
- Swapping 어느 for 어떤: saying '어느 사람이에요?' when asking what kind of person; 어떤 asks about kind/quality, while 어느 picks one out of a known set or means 'a certain'.
- Reading 어느 날 as a question ('which day?') instead of the storytelling meaning 'one day / some day', so they wrongly say '무슨 날 친구를 만났어요' for '어느 날 친구를 만났어요'.

## 뭐 / 어디 / 누구 as 'something / somewhere / somebody'
- Stressing the question word turns the sentence back into 'what/where/who' — mind your intonation.

## 아무나 / 아무도 (anybody / nobody)
- A sentence with 아무도 must end negatively — 집에 아무도 있어요 is wrong.
- Don't stack ~는/~가/~를 on top of ~나 or ~도.
- Remember the shortcut: positive ending → 아무나; negative ending → 아무도.

## 아무 + 거/데/때 + ~나 (anything / anywhere / anytime)
- Drop ~에 after 데 (아무 데나 가고 싶어요), but keep ~에서 before ~나 (아무 데서나 공부해요).
- 아무 것도 / 아무 데도 require a negative predicate.

## 아무 + ~나 / ~도 (any… / none…)
- Using 아무 데나 with an action verb like 먹다 — use 아무 데서나 when the verb needs ~에서.
- Saying '아무 때도' for 'at no time'; use 전혀 instead.
- Using 아무도 / 아무 것도 / 아무 데도 with a positive verb (아무도 그렇게 해요 is wrong) — these require a negative predicate.

## ~마다 (each / every)
- Writing 마다 as a separate word — it attaches directly to the noun.
- Confusing 마다 ('each', after the noun) with 모든 ('every', before the noun).

## ~에 vs ~에서 with places
- Using ~에서 with 가다 or ~에 with 먹다.
