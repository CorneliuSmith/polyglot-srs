# Common learner errors — Latin

- **Nominative/accusative confusion on neuters.** bellum stays bellum
  whether it's subject or object — learners over-apply the -um-changes-to-
  -am pattern they just learned for feminine nouns. Correct by naming the
  gender first, then the case.
- **Wrong principal part guessed from the wrong form.** Given only amō,
  learners guess the perfect is *amsi instead of amāvī. Always drill all
  four principal parts together (amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum), never the
  present alone.
- **Word-order literalism.** English SVO leaks in — learners assume Puella
  puerum amat must mean "the girl [is near] the boy [and] loves" in strict
  left-to-right reading instead of parsing the case endings. Rebuild the
  sentence by asking "which word has the -m?" before translating.
- **Case after two-way prepositions.** in + accusative (motion into) vs.
  in + ablative (location) gets flattened to "in always means in." Ask
  "is something moving, or just sitting there?" before picking the case.
- **Adjective agreement by proximity, not by referent.** Learners match an
  adjective's ending to the nearest noun rather than the noun it actually
  describes, especially when the two are split across the sentence for
  emphasis.
- **Dropping the pronoun and losing the person.** Since Latin usually omits
  subject pronouns, beginners sometimes default every verb to 3rd person
  because that's the "bare" form they memorized. Drill the person endings
  explicitly against amō/amās/amat.
